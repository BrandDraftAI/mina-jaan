The Wife He Never Met

Some vows come before the first hello.

Chapter 1 The Question

Sam had burned the peppers on purpose again and then spent ten minutes explaining that it was on purpose, and Ella let him get all the way to the end of it before she said that she knew, she’d watched him do it, she was standing right there.

“You could let me have the explanation.”

“I’ve had the explanation four times.”

“It gets better.” He put the plate down in front of her. “There’s a version where I bring in the science.”

“I’ve had the science.”

“There’s a version with charts.”

She ate the peppers. They were very good, which he knew, which was the whole reason for the ten minutes. Outside the kitchen window the light had gone the color it went in the last week of August, that thin gold that made the building across the alley look like it had been recently rained on whether or not it had. Somebody two doors down was running a table saw. It was seven o’clock on a Tuesday and there was nowhere either of them had to be.

He’d left the back door open. He always left the back door open, and every year around this week she started closing it behind him, and every year he opened it again, and this had been going on for four years without either of them saying a word about it directly. The staircase beyond it was the other joke. It had come with the building, wooden, unpainted, structurally fine according to two separate people they had paid to say so, and it made a noise like a question when anybody went down it. They had a running argument about whether it could be fixed. Neither of them wanted it fixed. Neither of them had ever admitted that, and by now the argument had outlived its own subject.

“Kaz came by,” Sam said. “About the shelf.”

“Which shelf.”

“The one you’ve been not asking me to put up.”

“I haven’t been not asking you. I’ve been going to do it.”

“You’ve been going to do it since June.”

“I’ve got a book due.” She heard how that sounded and kept going. “It’s on the list. It’s high on the list.”

“He said he could do it Saturday.”

“I don’t need Kaz to put up a shelf.”

Sam ate. He had a way of eating that used the whole fork and finished the sentence anyway, which she had noticed on their second date and had found irritating for about a year and then had stopped noticing at all until moments like this one, when he used it to avoid answering.

“He’s already coming for the gutter,” he said.

“Then he can do the gutter.”

“Ella.”

“I’m going to do the shelf.”

“Okay.”

“I am.”

“I said okay.” He reached over and took a pepper off her plate, which he was allowed to do and which she pretended he was not, and he ate it and did not look at her while he did it, and that was the end of it.

She thought about it for a while after, which was annoying. Not the shelf. The fact that she’d said no to a man coming to her house to do a job she didn’t want to do herself, and that Sam had let it go, and that underneath the letting go he’d understood exactly what she was doing and had decided not to make her look at it. He did that. It was one of the things she would have listed if anybody had asked her what was good about her marriage, and nobody ever did.

The deadline was nine weeks out. She’d said it that way to Bea in July, nine weeks, as though it were a long time, and Bea had said nothing in a manner that cost Ella most of an afternoon.

Ten books. She could do this. She knew where the weight sat in a book. She could take a manuscript apart on a train and tell you which chapter was carrying more than it could hold, and she had done it for other people’s books twice, for money, and been right both times. Structure was not the problem. The problem was in one room of the book the way a smell is in one room of a house, and she’d been opening every other door for five weeks.

“How’s the book,” Sam said.

“It’s fine.”

“It’s fine.”

“It’s going to be fine.”

“That’s a different thing.”

“It’s a version of the same thing.” She pushed the plate away an inch, which was the signal, and he did not take it. “It’s the middle. The middle’s always like this. You know it’s always like this.”

“I know you tell me it’s always like this.”

“That’s because it’s always like this.”

He let that sit. She waited for the follow-up, the one that would have been fair and that she had a whole answer prepared for, and it didn’t come.

That was the other thing she’d have listed. Six years, and she had never had to defend a bad week to him. Her mother had spent thirty years explaining herself at a kitchen table to a man who loved her and required the explanation anyway, and Ella had watched it, and had married somebody who asked once and then stopped, and had understood on some morning in the second year that this was not luck. He’d decided it. He decided things like that and never mentioned having decided them. He got up and put the pan in the sink and ran water into it and left it, which she would find in the morning and complain about, and would have missed if he’d washed it.

“Do you want the last of this,” he said, meaning the wine, holding it up.

“Half.”

He gave her more than half. She let him.

It was later, with the plates done and the radio on and the light gone, that she said the other thing. She hadn’t planned it. She’d planned it only in the sense that she had decided not to do it.

There was a woman in it who wanted something. That was the room with the smell in it, and she’d known that for five weeks, and she’d been circling it like a word she couldn’t spell. The woman wanted it and Ella couldn’t make her ask. Every draft where she asked read like a hostage tape. Every draft where she didn’t read like nothing at all, and nothing at all is a thing an editor can point to.

“Can I ask you something for work.”

“Sure.”

“It’s a research question.”

“You’ve never had a research question.”

“I have research questions all the time.”

“You have questions you’ve already answered and want me to agree with.” He was still at the counter, drying his hands on the towel that hung on the oven door, which was not a towel for hands, and she did not say so. “Go on.”

She’d had the sentence ready since Sunday. That was the part she’d have denied under oath. She’d built it in the shower and revised it twice on the walk back from the post office, and by Monday it had the shape of a thing you could say casually.

So she asked him. She asked whether there was a thing he’d ever wanted that he’d never said out loud, and she meant it in the way the book meant it, and it didn’t come out that way. It came out slower than she’d intended and pitched slightly wrong.

She’d left space at the end of it. She heard herself do that too.

Sam folded the towel in half and hung it back over the door.

“Sure,” he said. “A boat.”

“A boat.”

“A small one. Nothing crazy.”

“You get seasick in the car.”

“That’s why I’ve never said it out loud.”

She laughed. It was a good line and he’d found it fast, and she laughed for exactly as long as it deserved and then a half second past that, into the part where the laugh was doing something else, and then she stopped and picked up her glass and there was a silence with a shape to it.

“Do you want tea,” Sam said.

“No. Thanks.”

“I’m making it anyway.”

“Then yes.”

He filled the kettle. He did not look over. There was nothing in the way he stood at the counter, nothing at all, and she watched the back of him for a moment and then stopped watching. There is a difference between a person not saying something and a person having nothing to say. She had just been in a room with the first one and had chosen to treat it as the second.

The kettle got loud. The radio was doing the thing where the announcer talks over the end of the song. Somewhere below them the back staircase asked its question, once, which meant the wind had got under the door again, and neither of them got up.

“I’m going to work for a bit,” she said.

“Okay.”

“An hour.”

“Okay.”

She took the tea to the table and opened the laptop and sat in front of it with her hands flat on either side of it, and the cursor did what a cursor does. The woman still wanted it. Ella read the last paragraph she’d written on Friday, which ran four hundred words about a corridor. She’d put the woman in a corridor because she needed her somewhere and couldn’t stand to have her arrive.

In the other room Sam turned the radio down without being asked. Then, after a while, off. She heard him take the stairs to the second floor two at a time, the way he’d done at twenty-eight and had never stopped doing, and the house registered it in the frame, in the glasses on the shelf.

The pan would still be in the sink in the morning. She’d say something about it and he’d say something back and neither of them would mean any of it, and then it would be Wednesday.

She sat there for a long time. At some point she typed a sentence and looked at it and took it out again, and then she sat with her hands still on the table either side of the machine, not writing, and the house made the small settling sounds it made at that hour, and nobody in it said anything else at all.
