The Brother Nobody Forgave

Some debts are buried. Some brothers aren't.

CHAPTER 1

The last mile to the Marchesi house ran along the top of the headland. On the seaward side there was a low stone wall, and past the wall there was nothing for a long way down. Beppe took it at forty, and I’d driven this road myself in worse weather, in a Fiat with no second gear, and I’d have taken it faster.

The car wasn’t mine. That was the first thing. It was a gray saloon that smelled of someone else’s cigarettes and lemon polish, sent down the coast to collect me like a parcel. I’d written to Giulia that I could take the train and a taxi. She’d written back that it was no trouble, that the family insisted. Insisted. I’d had eight years of that word. You learned to let them.

“You’ve been up before, signorina,” Beppe said. It wasn’t a question.

“Four years ago. For the collection.”

“The silver.” He nodded at the windscreen. “Signora Giulia said you counted every spoon.”

“Two hundred and twelve,” I said. “One of them was plate.”

He laughed, then checked me in the mirror to see if I’d meant it. “Nobody told the Signore that, I hope.”

“It’s in the report. Page nine.”

The house came up on the left all at once, a long low block of pale stone laid across the highest point of the headland. Umbrella pines. A gravel sweep the size of a piazza. Below it, where the land fell toward the water, I could see the town, and past the town the three towers going up on the old marina with the cranes still on them. The Marchesi name ran along the hoarding at their base in letters you could read from here. They’d built most of what I could see from that road. In a fortnight, on a Friday, I was going to marry into it, and I’d spent eight years being grateful for the chance.

Beppe took my case. I took the ledger.

It was a real book, cloth-bound, ordered from a stationer who charged me thirty-eight euros because nobody wanted paper anymore. I kept my working notes in it. Inventories, valuations, measurements, maker’s marks, the clean square on a shelf where something used to stand. I wrote in a hand small enough to fit two lines where there was room for one. Families hired me so their lawyers could divide what they owned, or insure it, or marry it, or bury it. I was good at it because I didn’t want any of it. The ledger was why they’d first let me into this house, four years back, to value the collection. It was part of why Sandro had noticed me at all. A woman who could stand in a room full of their things and put a number on every one of them and want none of it. He told me later that was what he’d noticed first. He meant it as a compliment, and I let it be one.

“You made good time,” Sandro said, coming down the steps.

He was already smiling. He nearly always was, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. Staff stood straighter for it. Even now, knowing him, it made me feel for a second that the ground was solid. He kissed both my cheeks and held my shoulders at arm’s length to look at me. If you’d written a list of what a woman could want in the man she was marrying, Sandro Marchesi would have met it line by line. He was handsome without working at it. He was kind the easy way people are when they’ve never had to ration it. He had picked me out of a whole coast of women who’d have said yes faster, and he’d never once made me feel the distance between where I came from and where he stood.

“There’s a lot to get through,” I said. “I wanted the light.”

“Of course you did.” He took the ledger out from under my arm before I could stop him, weighed it in his hand, and gave it back. “Still writing it all down by hand. We have people for this, love.”

“You have people for everything,” I said. “That’s why you need me. Somebody has to know what’s actually here.”

He laughed. He liked it when I was dry with him. We went up the steps together into the cool of the house. The sea light came through the tall windows and lay across the floor in long panels. Terrazzo, original, with a patch near the door where someone had matched the chips badly in the seventies. I’d noticed it four years ago, and I noticed it again.

The job was real. That was what I held onto. Before a Marchesi married, the contract required a full inventory of the marital estate, everything the house held, listed and valued, so the lawyers could do whatever lawyers did with money old enough to have forgotten where half of it came from. Cesare had asked for it specifically. I was to have the run of the place, room by room, and produce the schedule in time for the signing. I’d done it a hundred times for strangers. Everyone thought it was charming that I was doing it for the family I was marrying. I thought it was a lot of rooms.

Cesare came out of the study as we crossed the hall. He always seemed to arrive a minute before you expected him, gray and upright, his jacket buttoned in his own house. He took my hand in both of his.

“Chiara. You found the road.”

“Beppe found it. I sat in the back.”

“Beppe has found it for thirty years.” He let go of my hand and took an envelope from his inside pocket. Cream laid paper, heavy, the firm’s square M embossed on the flap. “You’ll need this. The staff here know you. Down the coast they don’t, and I won’t have you stopped at a door by some boy with a lanyard.”

I opened it. One sheet, the firm’s letterhead, written in his own hand in black fountain pen. To all offices and staff of Marchesi Costruzioni. Signorina Chiara Bruno is to be given whatever access she requires for the inventory of the marital estate. His signature underneath, the C and the M run together.

“I wasn’t planning on the offices,” I said.

“Plan on everything. You always do.” He smiled. “The firm owns things the house has forgotten. A warehouse of my father’s furniture. Paintings in the boardroom nobody has looked at since they were hung. If it’s ours, I want it on your list.”

“Then it’ll be on my list.”

“Good.” He touched my shoulder, once, and went back into the study, and the door closed with a soft click.

I folded the letter in three and put it in the back of the ledger, behind the last page, where I kept receipts.

Giulia found us a minute later. Sandro’s mother moved through her own house quietly, touching nothing, a woman checking a museum after closing. She took my hands in both of hers and held them a moment too long. Her eyes went over my face.

“You’ve lost weight,” she said. “We’ll fix that this week.” Then lower, only to me: “You’re sure. About all of it.”

“Mamma,” Sandro said, mild.

“I only ask the once.” She squeezed my hands and let them go. “There. I’ve asked.”

I said I was sure. It wasn’t a lie. I’d priced this marriage, and it came out in my favor. My mother in a two-room flat with a balcony that the family kept for her, no rent. My sister Sofia at a university we could never have afforded, in her final year, ringing me on Sundays about boys and exams as if the money under it had always been there. Eight years ago it hadn’t been there. Eight years ago there had been a phone call in the middle of the night, and a man from the firm at our door before dawn. He was gentle, terribly gentle. He explained about the accident on the site, about my father, about how the family looked after its own. My father had worked for them nine years, so we were its own now, whether we’d earned it or not. There had been papers. I was nineteen. I signed them, and I was grateful, and I still was.

So. I was sure.

I started work that afternoon in the library and moved through the ground floor as the light over the water went gold and then red. It was good to have my hands busy. I measured, I noted, I wrote in the small hand. A pair of famille rose vases that weren’t a true pair, one with a hairline crack running up from the foot. Together, nine thousand at auction. Apart, a good deal less than half that each. A glass cabinet of medals belonging to a grandfather nobody mentioned. A Murano chandelier with three replacement drops, the new glass a shade too blue. It was the usual wealth of the very old rich, never as tidy as you’d think. A good Venetian mirror hung over a radiator cover somebody had ordered out of a magazine. Both had always been there. I filled four pages and felt myself settle.

There were cameras. I’d never let myself see them before, in four years of visits. In a house where you’re a guest you train your eyes past them, the same as you train them past the staff. That afternoon I saw them. A small dark eye in the corner of the library. Another over the main stairs. One outside on the eaves, which I found when I stepped onto the terrace to check the light on a painting.

It wasn’t pointed at the gate, or the road, or the long drive up from the headland. It was pointed in, along the house. It watched the terraces and the doors.

I stood under it with my tape measure in my hand, then under the one at the other end of the terrace. Same angle. I didn’t write the cameras down, because they weren’t the estate’s to value. Rented, I guessed, from a security firm with a contract and a monthly fee. I looked up at the first one twice more before the light went.

It was Beppe, carrying my case up to my room, who told me where not to go.

“Not the west end, signorina.” He said it over his shoulder, easily, a man pointing out a wet step. “The old wing. It’s shut up. You won’t need it.”

“The inventory’s meant to be everything.”

“It’s shut up,” he said again, and he looked at me pleasantly, and didn’t explain, and carried the case on up.

I went and looked, of course. At the western end of the long gallery a pair of double doors stood closed. They didn’t quite meet. Through the gap I could see the room beyond was full of white: furniture under dust sheets, shape after shape in pale cloth. A tall one, a wardrobe or a cabinet. Something low and square. Chairs, upside down on what had to be a table. I counted nine shapes before the gap ran out. Cold air came off the room and it smelled of stone. I stood there with my hand on the door and didn’t push. Then I went back down the gallery, took out the ledger, and wrote on a fresh line:

West wing, shut up. Not to be catalogued. Ask who decided.

I looked at it, then shut the book and went to change.

My mother rang while I was still in my slip. She rang at seven every evening I was away, as if seven were in the contract.

“You’re there,” she said.

“I’m here.”

“Did they send the car?”

“They sent the car. And a driver. He knows how many spoons there are.”

“Good.” I heard her kettle go on. She’d be standing at the counter by the window, her back to the room, because she never sat to talk on the phone. “Is the room warm? Those big houses, the walls hold the cold.”

“It’s warm, Mamma.”

“You took the good coat.”

“I took the good coat.”

“Wear it at the dinners. Not the black thing.” A pause while she poured. “How is Giulia?”

“She says I’ve lost weight.”

“You have.” Another pause. My mother could fit more into a pause than most people fit into a letter. “And Sandro.”

“He’s fine. He’s happy. Everyone’s happy.”

“Good,” she said again. “Eat what they give you, cara. Ring Sofia, she’s sulking that you didn’t ring her yesterday.” And then, because she never let a call run past what it cost: “Go on. You’ll be late down.”

I put the phone face down on the dressing table and stood looking at it for a minute before I went back to my hair.

Dinner was late. I braided my hair back off my face because I hated it loose over my hands. In the glass I saw the scar that split my left eyebrow, the one I told people came from a fall when I was small. I touched it once for luck, and went down.

They were in the long room off the terrace, and the doors to the sea were open. Cesare sat at the head of the table, gray and courtly. He was the man who had signed the papers that kept my mother housed, and he called me daughter now. Giulia was beside him. Sandro had a glass in his hand and his jacket off and he was laughing at something, the lamplight on him. A fortnight from a wedding, in the most beautiful house on the coast, marrying into the family that had saved mine. I stood in the doorway a moment before anyone saw me.

Then Sandro turned, still laughing, to say something to the man beside the terrace doors, and stopped.

I hadn’t seen him. He’d been in the room the whole time and I hadn’t seen him. He stood a little apart, near the open doors where the dark of the terrace came in, with his back not quite to the wall. From there he could see every way into the room. He wasn’t drinking. He wasn’t part of the laughing. He was watching the doorway I stood in. When I found him, he didn’t look away. He looked at me as though he already knew my name, and more than my name, and had come a long way to be here.

The laughing went out of the room. It didn’t stop all at once. Giulia’s voice trailed off in the middle of a word. Someone set a glass down on the sideboard too carefully. Cesare’s face closed. I had known him four years and I had never seen it do that. Sandro stepped in front of me, not quite between us, but nearly.

“You shouldn’t be here,” Cesare said. He didn’t raise his voice.

“No,” the man by the doors agreed. He still hadn’t looked away from me. “I know.”

Giulia had put her glass down half a second before anyone else. Cesare hadn’t moved at all. Sandro’s hand was at the small of my back, warm, where he always put it, and I was glad of it.

The man by the doors was still looking at me.

I’d always believed that if you knew exactly what was in a room, you were safe in it. My father could have told me otherwise. He stood exactly where the paper said it was safe to stand, and he knew that ground better than any man above him on that site, and it came down on him anyway.

Nobody said his name. Not one of them. I stood in the doorway with the sea air coming in behind me, in a room full of people I thought I knew, and every one of them had gone quiet around a man I had never met.
