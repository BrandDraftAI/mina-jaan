Kept

Some things own us long before we admit it.

Chapter 1

The studio was hers at six in the morning. That was most of the reason six in the morning existed. Maya had the block to herself on the walk over, the coffee place two doors down still dark, the light coming up gray above the parking structure across the street. She had her keys out before she reached the door, and then she stopped on the sidewalk with the ring hanging off one finger.

The alarm panel wasn’t blinking.

It always blinked. A small green pulse behind the glass, five mornings a week for six years. Now the panel sat dark. The deadbolt turned, the door swung open onto her own front room, and everything in it was where she’d left it. The easel under its drape. The long worktable with its jars of swabs and its magnifier on the swing arm. The two stools, one with the cushion she kept meaning to replace. The air wasn’t.

She stood in the doorway and didn’t go in. Cold air moved past her ankles from the back of the building, from a window that should have been latched. Under the solvent and the old varnish there was something else, faint, gone when she reached for it. Cigarettes, maybe. Or nothing.

She stood there long enough to feel foolish, keys in her hand, weight on the balls of her feet, the way she stood in front of a canvas she hadn’t earned an opinion on yet. Habit said walk in. Something older said wait. She gave it three seconds. The empty street behind her was not much company either.

“Right,” she said to the room, and went in.

The panel on the easel stopped her first, because it was still there. A Dutch workshop piece, oak, early seventeenth century, insured for more than the building, sitting under its cotton drape with the raking lamp cold beside it. She lifted the corner of the drape. The surface was as she’d left it, the retouching she’d spent three weeks on still tacky in one corner, the craquelure running true to the grain of the oak. Under the raking lamp a fingerprint in that varnish would have shown from ten feet. There was nothing. Whoever had come in had walked past six figures of somebody else’s money to get to something else. She let the drape fall.

She went front to back under her breath, the survey order she used for a damaged canvas. Pigments, present. Microscope, present. Both laptops. The good scope. The UV lamp. Petty cash in the tin that anyone with hands could have opened. The box of sable brushes on the shelf, the jar of swabs, the solvent cabinet with its key still in the lock. Her voice sounded strange in the empty room and she kept going anyway.

The back window told part of it. The latch had been cut, not forced, and there was one gray scuff on the sill where a shoe had come over. Below it the plan cabinet, where she kept the provenance archive, stood with its third drawer open two inches. That drawer stuck. It had stuck since she’d bought the cabinet at an estate sale her first year, and she had a way of lifting while she pulled that nobody else knew. Someone had forced it instead. The metal lip was bent where a bar had gone in.

She crouched at the sill and tilted her head until the weak light came across the cut at an angle, the same trick she used on a paint surface when a client swore nothing had ever been touched. Two clean passes. No hesitation marks, no second try. Somebody who had done this before, more than once, and didn’t need to think about it.

She pulled the drawer the rest of the way and the runner dropped off its track. The whole steel weight of it lurched and she caught it with her right hand under the corner. Something in her wrist went hot. She set the drawer on the floor, sat back on her heels, and held the wrist against her chest until the heat settled into an ache that ran to the elbow when she flexed. Fine. Wrapped, it would be fine.

The folders were the real information. She kept the archive by estate and then by acquisition date, in an order that lived in her hands more than in any index, and the order was gone. Not ransacked. Read. Each folder squared off and put back by someone who wanted them to look untouched and didn’t know the sequence, so the whole drawer sat a few degrees off true. The Copley estate folder was on top of the Wexford material. It had no business there.

She sat on the floor of her own studio with her wrist against her chest and looked at that for a while.

Then she did the practical things, because they were there to be done. She photographed the window, the latch, the bent lip, the scuff, and the drawer where it sat on the floorboards. She wrapped the wrist with cohesive bandage from the first aid tin, one-handed, teeth on the end of it. She put the kettle on, and while it heated she walked the front room again and found the second thing. Her appointment diary lay open on the desk. She always closed it. It was open to last month.

The kettle clicked off. She made tea and didn’t drink it.

Theo picked up on the second ring, so he was already at the shop with the door open for the early trade.

“It’s not Sunday,” he said, instead of hello.

“Somebody broke into the studio last night.”

She heard him put something down. “Are you hurt? Were you there?”

“I wasn’t there. I hurt my wrist this morning being stupid with a drawer. Theo, they didn’t take anything.” She looked at the drape while she said it. “They came in the back, went through my files, and left. The painting’s here. The laptops are here.”

“Files,” he said.

“The provenance archive.”

He didn’t say anything. She knew that pause. Kitchens with the radio turned up. Their mother saying not on the phone. When he spoke again his voice was reasonable.

“Okay. So you call the insurance company. You get the window fixed today, you get a better alarm. Sal at the hardware store can put a bar on that window by tonight. I’ll call him.”

“Thank you,” Maya said. Somebody else was already reaching for a phone. She could have sat down on the floor again.

Then she said, “And the police?”

“Maya.”

“I’m asking what you think.”

“Yeah. I mean, you know what I think.” He said it gently. “You file a report, some detective types your name in, and it’s not your name that comes up. It’s Dad’s. And then it’s a whole thing.” He stopped there. “Nothing got taken. Fix the window.”

She turned the mug a quarter turn on the desk and looked at the diary, still open to the wrong month, and didn’t answer until he said her name again.

“I heard you.” She turned the mug back. “I’m not calling the police. You’re right about that part.”

“Good.” He breathed out. “You had me for a second. I thought I was calling Sal for nothing.”

“You are calling Sal for nothing. I already yelled at the window myself. Very sternly.”

“Uh huh.”

“I’m going to hire somebody, though.”

“A locksmith. Yeah. Good.”

“Security. The private kind.” She picked up a pencil and put it down. “Somebody reads my files and leaves a painting worth more than this building. That’s not a burglar having an off night, Theo. That’s somebody who wanted to know something. I want to know what before they come back.”

He was quiet. She could hear the shop around him, the door chime, a customer asking about a drop-leaf, the radio low on the bench. Ordinary Monday. “Hang on.” Away from the phone: “No, that one’s sold, sorry, she’s picking it up Thursday. The leaves stick. I told her they stick.” Back to her. “Okay. Sorry. Those guys aren’t cheap, Maya. And they’re gonna dig, right? That’s the whole job. Same stuff a detective would.”

“Then at least I’ll be paying the person who reads it.”

He laughed once, through his nose. “Mom used to say you’d argue with a locked door.”

“The door lost. I have to go. I’ll call you Sunday.”

“Okay. Sunday,” Theo said.

He let her have the last word.

She set the phone down and sat with her wrapped wrist in her lap, and then she got up.

The insurance company took forty minutes and put her on hold twice. The adjuster kept asking what was taken, ma’am, in a voice that didn’t believe the answer. The glazier came at noon and charged her for the emergency. Sal arrived while the glazier was still packing up, looked at the cut latch for a long time, and said it was a clean job, whoever it was.

“You want it flush or proud?” he said, holding the bar up against the frame.

“I don’t know what either of those means.”

He nodded, as if that was correct. Sal believed nobody who hired him should know anything about his trade. “Flush,” he decided. “So it doesn’t catch,” and started drilling before she could agree.

She stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and watched his hands. They didn’t go wrong. He asked if she wanted the front window barred too, while he had the ladder out, and she said yes to the ladder and paid him in cash from the tin the burglar hadn’t wanted. When he packed his kit the light outside had gone flat and white. Nothing left to rake.

By four the studio looked like itself again. She didn’t trust it. She sat at the desk with the wrist on the wood and went through the archive drawer properly, folder by folder. Hartline. Wexford. Delacroix-Munn, which had never been a Delacroix, whatever the family’s lawyers preferred. Copley, Beckstead, the Aldous consignment with its water damage and its lies. Twenty-six folders, six years of deaths and divorces and quiet sales, and her hands knew the thickness of each one. She checked the corners. She checked the order of the sheets inside. She checked the staples, which she always set at the same slant in the top left corner, and the sticky flags on the condition reports, yellow for open questions, green for closed.

The paperclip rust on the second Hartline sheet was still where it always sat. She had never moved that clip.

The wrist put up a line of heat when she put the last folder straight. She sat back and watched the new glass in the back window hold the gray afternoon. Then she put Copley back where it belonged. She closed the diary and put it in a different drawer. Then she called Iman.

“You never call on a workday,” Iman said. “Someone died or someone attributed a Rembrandt.”

“Someone broke into my studio and read my files.”

“Read them.”

“Read them and left. Left the Beckstead panel on the easel.”

Iman whistled, low. They’d been taking work off each other’s desks for ten years and arguing attribution at conferences in front of people who enjoyed it too much. There was no one else in the field Maya would have called.

“You want my insurer’s investigator,” Iman said.

“Better than that. You did the Hartline job, the place out in the county with all the security. Who ran the protection?”

“Meridian. Meridian Protective. Transport and site work. And I’ll tell you what I noticed, which is that I didn’t notice them. Quiet people. The one who ran it checked every room he walked into like he was pricing the exits.” A beat. “You’d hate him or you’d hire him. Possibly both.”

Maya wrote the name on the pad by the phone in block letters and underlined it. “Send me the number.”

“You’re actually doing this. Your brother must be thrilled.”

“My brother wants me to buy a better lock.”

“Your brother restores furniture for people who want the damage to disappear. You and I make it visible and charge more. Send me a photo of the latch. I want to see the cut.”

Maya sent it. Iman’s answer came back before she’d put the phone down. Ooh. Two passes. Professional. Call Meridian.

◆

The Meridian intake line was answered by a woman named Dana who took the facts without comment, asked about the street and the neighbors and the hours Maya kept, and moved through her questions fast enough that Maya was answering before she’d decided how much to say. Entry point: the back window, already fixed. Anything disturbed, even with nothing taken: Maya described the drawer. Then, did anyone besides her hold a key to the building.

“Only me,” Maya said, half a second late.

Dana didn’t remark on it. The word retainer came up. Maya gave her card number, her own card, the account with nothing of the family in it, and listened to a confirmation being read back. Consultation at nine tomorrow, on site, with the principal. Outside the light went from gray to the colder blue just before the streetlamps.

The confirmation email arrived while she was still holding the phone. Three lines and a reference number, signed by nobody. She looked the firm up. Four pages that said almost nothing. No staff photographs, no client list, the services written by somebody who didn’t like adjectives, an address in a part of town where the buildings didn’t bother with signs. The principal appeared once, at the bottom, name only.

N. Anand.

She said it out loud to the empty studio and tried the initial a few ways. Nathan. Nicholas. Nobody. It got her about as far as the scuff on the sill. She closed the laptop.

She should have gone home. Instead she turned off the desk lamp and stood in the middle of the room while it went to shapes around her, solvent and new putty in the air. The painting under its drape, worth a house. The laptops, worth a used car. The tin, worth a decent dinner. The drawer of paper, worth nothing to anyone.

Someone had stood here in the dark last night, close enough to touch the drape. He’d had good tools and a whole night. He had left the house and the car and the dinner where they were, and read twenty-six dead people’s paperwork, and put it back almost right. Somebody knew which folder sat where in this drawer now. Theo didn’t know that. Nobody did but her, and now him.

Her wrist ached when she turned the deadbolt. Full dark already, her breath showing in the doorway light. The coffee place had closed again and the block was dark on both sides. She left the light on over the door, for no reason she could have defended. She walked home with her collar up, past windows going yellow one at a time, and tried to think what kind of thief brings good tools and patient hands and a whole night, and spends it reading.

At her own building she didn’t check the street behind her, and noticed herself not checking. The radiator ticked. She ate half a sleeve of crackers standing at the counter and ran the wrist under warm water longer than it needed and watched the wrap darken. Copley on top of Wexford. Theo’s voice going careful. She slept, eventually, not well.
