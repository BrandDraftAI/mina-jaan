Ghostwritten

Some names disappear. Others are erased.

Chapter 1

The last book Reese Vaughn wrote for Aldieri Press ended her career, and this morning the house wanted her back in the building by ten. She got there at nine twenty. Verifiers run early. A room tells you more before it starts performing, and she wanted the lobby’s number before the lobby had hers.

Her phone went off against her ribs while she was still across the street, reading the facade the way she’d read a first edition somebody swore was authentic. Arjun. She let it ring twice and answered.

“Tell me you’re standing outside,” Arjun said. “Tell me you didn’t get to the corner and turn around.”

“I’m outside.”

“And you’re going in.”

“I’m checking the source. Houses that blacklist you don’t send an offer five years later unless something’s wrong with the offer.”

“Something is wrong with the offer, Reese. The man died before he finished his book. That’s the wrong part. That’s the whole job.” He was trying not to sound hopeful on her behalf and not managing it. “Edmund Vane. The estate, the vault, full access, and a fee with a comma in it where your fees haven’t had a comma since I’ve known you. You want this. I can hear it from here.”

“I don’t have a tone.”

“I didn’t say tone. You said tone. You’ve got the voice you use when you’ve already said yes and you’re waiting for somebody to talk you out of it, and I’m not going to, so don’t ask. Go in. Sign the thing. And don’t stand in their lobby working out why they picked you. Do the job first, work it out after, call me, I’ll be here, I’m always here.”

She crossed with the light. She’d already worked it out, all night at her kitchen table, and the answer was short. Vane’s memoir was the estate sale of the decade. Every ghost with a clean name would have taken it for the scale alone, and the house had reached past all of them for the one name on the industry’s quiet list. Offers like that weren’t kindness. She’d said yes in four minutes anyway.

The lobby smelled like a library that had married money, paper and lilies and floor wax, and the kid at the front desk found her name on the first try without making her spell it. Fourth floor. Someone would come down.

It wasn’t an assistant who came. The man waiting when the elevator opened wore a gray suit half a shade darker than hers and stood very still, and she knew his face from the back pages of other people’s launch coverage: Luca Aldieri, literary executor, the house’s own blood. He’d come down himself for a ghost. She filed it.

“Ms. Vaughn.” His handshake was dry and brief. “Luca Aldieri. Thank you for coming to us.”

“You came down yourself.”

“I prefer to collect things personally. They arrive in better condition.” He said it the way other men hold a door. “This way.”

She wasn’t, so far as she knew, a thing. She followed him anyway, past a floor of editors who looked up in the practiced sequence of people who’d been told not to look up. Every one of them knew who she was. Her name had gotten here first.

His office was the corner, old glass, an older desk, and a single neat stack of paper in the middle of the blotter with a pen laid beside it at a right angle. No folder of pleasantries. The contract first.

“Coffee?” he said.

“After the definitions.”

“A woman who reads the definitions.” He sat across from her, not behind the desk, and set his watch face down on the wood. “Take whatever time it costs.”

She read the way she read everything, definitions forward, signature page backward, and he sat through all seventeen pages of it without touching a phone, without straightening anything, without the small noises men make when a woman is taking too long. Then she read it again. He waited through that too.

The terms were clean where she expected clean and sharp where she expected sharp. Work in the vault only. No original leaves it, not a page, not a photograph, not a phone. The dictation transcribes offsite, digitized only, on the estate’s license, because nobody transcribes forty hours standing up in a coat. The fee in thirds. Her name nowhere, ever, in any edition, in any territory, in perpetuity. Clause fourteen let the house end her at will, any reason or none, everything unpaid forfeit. She read clause fourteen twice.

“The waiver of attribution runs both directions,” he said when she paused on it. “You can’t claim the work, and the estate can’t name you, even in the acknowledgments. It’s standard.”

“I know standard. I’ve been standard for five years.” She initialed the clause without slowing. He didn’t make the sympathetic noise. Everybody made the noise.

“Eight weeks,” he said. “The estate announces in the fall and wants pages before it does. The vault will open seven days a week until you’re finished. The schedule isn’t generous, and I won’t pretend otherwise.”

“I’ve done faster for worse. One addendum.” She turned the contract around on the wood. “A dated copy of my working notes goes to the estate’s counsel every Friday. Sealed. Not for credit. For provenance.”

He didn’t ask why. “Done,” he said, and wrote the rider into the margin himself, initialed it, and turned it back. It took him four seconds. She noticed how fast he gave away the small thing and wondered where he kept the large ones.

“Why me?”

She watched him decide how much of the answer to spend.

“Because the voice is the asset.” He said it without ceremony. “The estate holds eleven hundred pages of draft, forty years of letters, nine notebooks, and no ending. Any good writer could finish the book. You’d finish it in his voice instead of yours, because you treat voice as evidence, not decoration. I’ve read your books, Ms. Vaughn. The credited ones sound like their authors and nothing like each other. That’s rarer than this trade admits.”

Your books. Plural, possessive. Men that careful didn’t misplace pronouns. Nobody had said it to her in five years.

“Who had it before me?”

“Two writers the estate liked and I didn’t. They wrote Vane the way he sounded at banquets.” He straightened the pen half a degree, the first motion he’d wasted all morning. “The banquet voice is a costume. If you don’t know that, you’re no use to him.”

“And the house’s exposure?” she said. “My name comes with a file.”

“I’ve read the file too. The exposure is priced in.” No flinch she could source. “You’ll work in a sealed room, on paper that never leaves, under a camera, beside an archivist who’s caught better forgers than either of us will ever be. The vault doesn’t run on trust, and neither do I. You may find that restful.”

The file. Five years ago her name had sat on a different Aldieri contract, a dozen books into a career of sounding like people who paid to be more interesting than they were, and then a paragraph surfaced in a book she’d ghosted, a small confident lie about a person she’d never met, sitting in a chapter she’d sourced twice. The credited author was shocked, in writing, on letterhead. It took the trade one week to decide whose fault a ghost’s book is.

Nobody sued her. A suit would’ve made her real. Her calls just started reaching assistants, then voicemail, then nothing, and the money that year went to lawyers who advised her to sign language she hadn’t written about language she hadn’t written. She never signed it. Since then she checked every source twice and didn’t drink water she hadn’t poured herself.

“Restful,” she said, “is a strange sales pitch.”

“I don’t pitch. I disclose.”

She picked up his pen. A fountain pen, heavier than it looked, still warm from his hand. She signed all four flags and didn’t think about the warmth.

Her signature had gotten smaller over five years. She watched it dry on his paper.

He watched her do it. Not the way legal watches, for completeness, and not the way editors watch, already spending you. She knew the usual kinds of attention, and his wasn’t one of them. She couldn’t read it, and she could read anything. That night she wrote both facts down in the notebook that never left her bag.

He signed beneath her without sitting back down and dated both copies, and for a moment the two names sat on the page together, hers above his. Neither of them said anything about the order.

“The vault’s two floors under the street,” he said, handing her a copy. “Sofia Reyes runs it. Below the street her rules outrank mine, and she enjoys that more than she’ll ever show you.”

The elevator was an old brass cage that wanted his badge and then a second badge, and the air cooled on the way down. Neither of them filled the ride. His sleeve was four inches from her shoulder. She spent the floors reading the inspection certificate, which was current, and which was the only thing in that cage she could verify.

Sofia Reyes waited at a steel door with a sign-in sheet on a clipboard, sixty maybe, silver hair cropped close, glasses on a chain. She looked at Reese like a document. It was the friendliest thing that had happened to her all morning.

“Pencils,” Sofia said, in place of hello. “No ink past this door, no bags, no coats, phone in the tray. You get the north table, under the camera. You photograph nothing, you move nothing between folders, and if something’s filed wrong you bring it to me, you don’t fix it.”

“Understood.”

“Everybody says understood.” Sofia held the tray out. “Then they clip pages. Clip nothing.”

“I count corners at the end of every session,” Reese said, surrendering the phone. “You’re welcome to count behind me.”

Sofia looked at her half a second longer, recalibrating. “The north table,” she said, which from a certain kind of woman is a love letter.

“The locker’s yours for the duration,” Sofia said, leading her in. “Vane gets pulled two boxes at a time and goes back the same day. He doesn’t sleep out on the table.”

“Neither will I.”

“They all say that too.”

“Six o’clock, the floor locks,” Luca said from the anteroom, already turning to go. “I’ll leave you to him.” It took her half a beat to hear that the him was the dead man.

Then the door. Steel shelving ran off under low even light, gray boxes lined up flush with the shelf fronts, and the cold had a hum in it you felt in your fillings more than heard. It smelled of cold paper. She liked it at once.

Vane had a range to himself, eleven bays of steel, a hundred and twelve boxes in Sofia’s block capitals, EDMUND VANE PAPERS, numbered in order. She approved. The working typescript lived in boxes forty-one and forty-two, nearest the table, under a finding aid in an oatmeal folder, every item numbered, every number initialed. The dictation tapes he’d used at the end were listed on the last page, digitized, an afterthought with its own appendix. She signed for the appendix before she signed for anything else. Forty hours of a dead man talking was the only part of this job the house was going to let her take home, and she meant to have it by the weekend.

She didn’t read first. Counting came first. That was the oldest rule she had, older than the blacklist, learned at the register of her grandparents’ nursery on San Pablo Avenue, where the till got counted twice at close, once in English, once in Japanese, because Grandma Tanaka trusted arithmetic and no particular language. You count what you were given before you fall in love with what it says.

The first page got her anyway. Vane on paper sounded exactly like Vane at a podium, one long courtly sentence going out and out until you were sure he’d dropped it, then the last four words landing like a check you didn’t know you were owed. She sat in the cold and heard a dead man breathe between clauses.

The draft opened with the century and traveled it first class, presidents and poets name-checked with the same fork. Vain, funny, expensive. And unfinished in the exact way that ruins books, the voice all there, the last rooms empty. She caught herself drafting his sentences in her head before she’d finished the second box.

The notebooks were another man entirely, blunt pencil, no audience, grocery lists sharing pages with paragraphs that would’ve won prizes if he’d ever let them out of the pen. She liked him better there.

By half past four she’d mapped boxes one through six and ruled her count sheet into the grid she’d used for five years: eleven hundred and forty draft pages in fair order, nine notebooks, correspondence sampled and left for tomorrow. The vault door sighed a little after that, and Luca set a coffee on the ledge in the anteroom, the side of the glass where liquids were still legal. “You cleared the definitions,” he said.

“You remembered the coffee.”

“You said after the definitions.” He stayed on his side of the ledge, hands in his pockets, reading her table through the glass. “May I ask what you’re counting?”

“What the estate gave me. Pages today, sense tomorrow.”

“Most writers would start with the sense.”

“Most writers trust pages.” She closed the folder she’d finished. “I stopped.”

He looked at her table a moment longer. “Sofia locks at six,” he said. “The coffee doesn’t come in. Her floor, her law. Good night, Ms. Vaughn.” He went up the stairs without hurrying, and the vault was hers and the dead man’s again.

At ten to six she reached the front matter Vane had typed himself, and there it was, a table of contents in his hand-corrected type, twenty-two chapters, each with its first line typed beside it. She checked it against the count sheet, and the count sheet against the boxes. The typescript ran twenty-one.

Chapter nineteen sat in the contents and nowhere else. No folder, no carbon, no title page, not a leaf of it anywhere in either box. The finding aid called the run complete, twenty-one folders, over somebody’s initials, and the pagination never blinked, eighteen handing straight to twenty as if nineteen had never been assigned a page in its life. Next to nineteen, its first line had been typed over with a row of lowercase x’s, the way machines used to take things back. She emptied both boxes and refilled them in order. Nothing.

There were nine ordinary explanations, and she could list them without lifting her pencil. Ten, counting spite. Authors cut chapters. Assistants renumber. Front matter outlives every plan a book ever makes. Vane had been ninety-one and dying, and dying men change their minds about what goes in the box. It was a clerical gap, the kind estates shed the way trees shed in October, and she’d found a dozen like it in a dozen better-kept rooms.

She logged it flat and dated and sourced: box forty-one, front matter, item six, contents lists twenty-two chapters with first lines, typescript holds twenty-one, chapter nineteen not located, query the archivist. Her pencil stayed even.

At six the buzzer went, polite and final. She packed the boxes back in their order, counted corners for Sofia’s camera, signed the sheet, took her phone out of the tray, and drank Luca’s coffee cold on the legal side of the glass with her coat over her arm. Twenty-two chapters in the dead man’s plan. Twenty-one in his boxes. She’d ask Sofia in the morning, and there’d be a form and an answer and somebody’s initials.

On the sidewalk her phone came back to life with three messages from Arjun, each one longer than the last. She typed back one word, signed. His reply was a photograph of a raised glass, inside four seconds, which meant he’d been sitting there holding it since ten.

The evening had gone gold on the better side of the street, and she walked to the train with the contract in her bag. Everything in that vault had a number, an initial, a reason. Nineteen had only its number. On the train she opened her notebook to the entry and read it again, and two stops later she opened it and checked that it was still there.
