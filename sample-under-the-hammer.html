Under the Hammer

Some treasures were never meant to be found.

Chapter 1

Marisol had photographed the contract twice before she signed it, once for the file and once because some habits didn’t care whether they were useful. The second photo caught nothing the first hadn’t. She kept it anyway.

Haddad’s took up most of a block on the east side, limestone and glass and a revolving door that turned itself before she reached it. Inside, the ceiling ran three stories up to a skylight, and directly under the skylight sat the block: a low wooden podium worn pale in one spot the size of a hand, where three generations of Haddad auctioneers had rested a palm between lots. The wood in that spot had worn down past the varnish. She’d read about the block in two histories of the house. She hadn’t expected it to be so small. She stood looking at it long enough that a porter had to step around her with a crate marked FRAGILE in three languages.

Six weeks. That was the whole contract, the third week of September to the last week of October, a week short of the sale itself, less than she’d have liked and more than her firm thought the fee justified. She’d taken it the day the draft catalog crossed her desk and she got to lot 118: three jade carvings, filed under a private continental collection, no further name attached. No attribution. A phrasing so careful it read like somebody’s decision rather than somebody’s ignorance.

Her phone buzzed. Her mother, checking whether she’d landed. She typed back that she had, that the building hadn’t fallen down around her yet, and put the phone away before she could look at the other photo saved next to the contract, the one that was seven small carvings and a woman’s hand and a date written on the back in her grandmother’s script. Not yet. She had six weeks. She could ration herself that much.

“Ms. Chen.” The woman crossing the lobby had a lanyard, a tablet, and the look of someone who had already decided something about the morning. “Kavya Nathan, head registrar. You’re early.”

“Bad habit.”

“We’ll see.” Kavya said it lightly, but her eyes went to the visitor badge printing behind the desk as if she was hoping it would take longer. “This way. I’ll get you set up with the systems you’ll need. Not all of them today.”

The registrar’s office sat two floors below the saleroom, fluorescent light and steel shelving, condition reports stacked in a queue that never seemed to shrink. Nobody explained the order of the tour. Kavya walked her through the intake database first, a search screen older than it should have been for a house this size, then the photography protocol binder, thick with amendments in three different hands. At the loupe station by the north window, where the light came in clean and cold, Kavya pulled a small porcelain dish off the shelf and set it down without a word.

Marisol turned it once under the light, checked the glaze pooling at the foot rim, and set it back down. “Kangxi mark, Republic-period body. Somebody’s grandmother’s dish, not the emperor’s.”

“The last specialist we brought in called it genuine.”

“The last specialist you brought in was wrong, and I’d bet money he never turned it over.” She didn’t say it unkindly. Kavya looked at the dish, then at her, and made a note on the tablet.

It was a better setup than the last two houses Marisol had worked. The binder had been amended by people who read it. Somebody here cared about getting the job right, or had cared once.

“And the confidential division,” Marisol said, when the tour reached the end of the hallway and turned back the way they’d come. “Private sales. I’ll need visibility into any lot that’s been through consignment history there, if it touches anything I’m authenticating.”

Kavya’s tablet stayed where it was. “That access gets requested separately.”

“By who.”

“By whoever’s chairing the file.” She said it smoothly, a sentence she had said before. “I can put in the request. It usually takes a few days to clear. Sometimes longer.”

“My contract closes in six weeks.”

“I’m aware of the calendar.” Kavya’s phone lit up in her hand. She looked at it, didn’t answer it, and put it face down on the counter. “Let me see what I can do.”

Past the loupe station a service corridor turned off toward a heavier door, steel instead of glass, with a keypad and a camera mounted above it at an angle that caught the whole hallway. Kavya didn’t slow down. “Storage vault. Temperature-controlled, dual-key access, logged in and out. You’ll be down there plenty once your own lots clear intake.”

She didn’t say when that would be.

A voice came down the hall, warm and unbothered, and Kavya stood up straighter before the man came into view.

“Kavya. Tell me you haven’t already lost our new specialist to the filing system.”

“Working on it, Mr. Haddad.”

“Rafe.” He was tall without making anything of it, dark hair going silver early at one temple only, a suit that fit as if it had been cut for this hallway. He looked at Marisol the way a man looks at a lot he hasn’t decided the estimate on yet. “Ms. Chen. I hope Kavya’s version of the tour includes coffee somewhere.”

“It includes a loupe station and a filing cabinet full of forms.”

“That’s the abridged version. The full one has coffee.” He glanced at Kavya. Then, as if it were nothing: “The confidential division access. Give her whatever she needs to cross-reference against the public catalog. I’ll sign off.”

Kavya’s jaw moved. “That’s not usually how the request goes.”

“It’s how it’s going today.”

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Kavya nodded once, made a note, and went down the hall toward the intake room, already dialing before she was out of earshot.

Rafe watched her go. “This way,” he said. “I’ll walk you through the rest myself.”

“You’ve read my file.”

“I read everyone’s file. Yours was more interesting than most.” He took her up a back stair instead of the elevator, unhurried, at a pace that assumed she’d keep up. “The Fitzgerald Ming. The house that hired you tried to bury the correction.”

“It wasn’t a difficult call once you had the tool marks under decent light.”

“It cost you the client.”

“It cost them a lawsuit, eventually. I’m still working.”

He nodded as though she’d confirmed a figure. “And a repatriation matter before that. Your name’s on the filing. The settlement’s sealed.”

“You read further than most people bother to.”

“I like to know who I’m handing a key to.” He said it lightly, and watched her face while he said it.

The stair let out onto a mezzanine that ran along the top of the saleroom, glass-fronted offices on one side, the block visible below through the rail. Rafe’s office was the last door along. Beside it hung a framed document, the ink gone the color of weak tea: an incorporation filing, a date in the early sixties, and across the top a name that read Haddad-Duarte Fine Arts before the paper’s crease cut the rest off. Rafe walked past it.

Inside, the office was glass on two sides, with a desk that had three things on it: a lamp, a ledger bound in cracked green leather, and a small brass hand bell that looked older than the building. He waved her toward a chair she didn’t take yet and set a shallow velvet tray on the desk between them.

“There’s a Wanli-period jade on the block next month,” he said. “Nothing to do with your contract. I’d like your opinion anyway, if you have four minutes.”

The tray held a bracelet, pale green shading to white at one edge, with a hairline where it had once been broken and reset so cleanly it took her a second look to find it. She didn’t touch it yet. She took out her phone, photographed it from the left, then from the right, and only then reached for the gloves folded in her bag.

“You always do that,” he said.

“Before I say anything I can’t take back, yes.”

She turned the bracelet once under the north light and found the tool marks along the inner curve, and the slight give in the stone that a good copy never quite got right however well it was carved. She checked the weight in her palm, then the temperature against her wrist, the small dull tests that never made it into a catalog description. “Late Ming, not Wanli specifically. The repair’s older than the crack. Somebody fixed this a hundred years before your consignor found it, which means whoever sold it to your consignor either didn’t know or didn’t say.”

“Which.”

“I’d need the paperwork to tell you that.” She set the bracelet back in its tray with both hands. “You could ask the confidential division. If they’re the ones who touched it before it came to you.”

His jaw set for half a second, then let go. “I’ll look into it,” he said, and reached for the ledger.

She stood to go. At the door he said her name, and when she turned back he was still behind the desk, one hand flat on the ledger’s cover.

“Have you worked a house’s confidential division before? Not the public catalog. The other side of it.”

“No,” she said. “Should I have?”

He held her look a second longer than the question needed. “Kavya will have your access sorted by tomorrow,” he said, and opened the ledger.

She went down the back stair past the framed document with its cut-off name, past the block with its pale worn spot under the skylight, and out through the revolving door.

It was colder than when she’d gone in. Traffic ran the avenue in both directions, a bus kneeling at the stop, a cab leaning on its horn at nobody in particular. She stood on the curb and let the noise fill her ears after an hour of quiet rooms. She got two blocks before she took her phone back out, past the contract photo, to the one underneath it: seven carvings, a woman’s hand, a date in her grandmother’s writing that nobody in the family had said out loud in fifty years.

Her grandmother had never once used the word stolen. Sold, she’d said, on the two occasions she’d said anything at all, and then she’d changed the subject to the weather or the price of rice until it stayed changed. Marisol had grown up believing the jade was simply gone, the way old things went, lost somewhere between a country and a decade. It had taken her own training, years of tool marks and repair lines, before she started to wonder who had decided it was gone.

Six weeks to find out whether the building behind her held the answer. She put the phone away before she could look too long, and did not, this time, photograph it twice.
