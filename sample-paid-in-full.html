Paid in Full

Every debt remembers.

Chapter 1

The engagement letter had one name on it, and the name was hers.

Naomi read it twice at the kitchen table, once for the terms and once for the feeling of it, which she did not trust. Reyes Forensic, the letterhead said, in a typeface she had chosen at two in the morning eight months ago and still was not sure about. Below it, the scope of work, the fee, the client. Sallowmere Capital Partners, care of an attorney named D. Osei. She had negotiated the number up twice and Osei had paid it both times without a word. That was the first thing about this job that kept her up.

The negotiation had taken four emails and nine days. She had asked for a third above her sheet rate, and Osei had replied inside the hour with a revised letter, the number already amended. He had drafted both versions before he asked her anything. She raised it again on scope. The second yes came faster than the first, and the retainer landed by wire the next morning, a day before the letter required it. She wrote that down, and under it paid early, why, and under that the date the wire landed.

The second thing was the amount. It would clear her mother’s second mortgage with enough left to breathe. She had run that arithmetic so many times it had stopped being numbers and become a picture: her mother’s hands not doing the thing they did when the statements came, the small folding motion, the putting away. Naomi initialed the bottom of the letter and dated it beside her name. Then she noticed she had done it to her own engagement letter, and left the initials there anyway.

Lorna from her old firm had called it a bad idea in three separate registers. Too big for a solo shop. Too clean a fee for a client that quiet. Too much of Naomi’s year riding on one engagement, with no partner behind her and no name but her own on the door if it went wrong. “Who stands behind you if it goes wrong?” Lorna had asked, the third time. Naomi had said, “The report,” and Lorna had laughed, and then stopped laughing when Naomi didn’t. All of that was true. What Naomi told herself was that she had spent six years working for people who would have made her soften a finding and was not going to spend a seventh, and that if the job was hers alone, then so was the number in the report, whatever it turned out to be.

She made coffee she did not need and opened the first box.

Sallowmere had sent the production the way rich clients always did, everything at once, so the weight of it would say cooperate without anyone having to. Naomi unpacked it in intake order, a pencil number on the corner of each document and a line in her index for each number. The intake took two hours, and the boxes smelled of toner and cardboard the whole time: formation documents, three years of statements, the subsidiary chart folded twice because it did not fit a page. She laid the chart flat, put a mug on one corner, and read it for who had been in the room when it was drawn. You could tell where a founder had been careful, where he had been frightened, where he had gotten greedy and stopped hiding it. Sallowmere’s chart was careful at the top and careful in the middle. Then, at the bottom, in the third tier of subsidiaries where nobody was supposed to look, it changed hands.

The tiers above were named the way men name things they are proud of, initials and one joke that had survived the lawyers. Tier three went flat. The names down there were built to pass a tired eye, and the formation dates all fell inside the same week: one drafting session, one drafter. It bothered her that she admired it. The addresses repeated and then did not. The holding company at the bottom had been formed by a firm whose style she knew.

She knew it because she had worked next to it once.

Naomi got up and stood at the counter with her back to the table and her palms flat on the cold laminate, and left them there. Outside, a truck took the corner too fast and somebody’s dog had opinions about it. Her apartment was small and very clean and held almost nothing she would have missed, which she had told herself for years was discipline. Bel called it a hostage situation. On the windowsill sat the one bowl she had kept from the Tuesday class, lopsided, glazed a color she had been aiming to make ugly and had missed. In the drawer under it, still in the box, still wound down to nothing, lay her father’s watch, handed to her at twenty-two with the words wear it or don’t and never once worn. She looked at the bowl and not at the box and gave herself a full minute.

Then she went back and confirmed it.

The firm that formed the tier-three holding company was Pellam and Croft. Not the whole firm: one associate’s drafting hand, the particular way this person built a shell so that it read empty from above and had a floor you could stand on from inside. The tells were small. A purpose clause wide enough to drive anything through, worded so blandly that nobody reviewing it ever slowed down. In the definitions, a recycled clause with its numbering off by one, an error a decade old and uncorrected, because correcting it would admit the whole template was one template.

Naomi had learned to read that hand at twenty-seven, in a glass conference room with bad air. The partner’s pen tapped. The carpet smelled of a floor cleaned at midnight by people nobody on the deal ever saw. Her reflection doubled in the glass wall, one of her hanging out over the city and one laid flat across the table, both of them looking at a clearance for the Halloran fund’s books. There was one line in it she had wanted to run down and had not. Running it down meant another week, and the partner said the deadline was the client and the client was the deadline, and he had a way of making a question sound like a failure of nerve. “Initial it, Naomi,” he had said. “We’ll footnote it next year.” There was no footnote the next year. Naomi was tired and junior and wanted to be the kind of person who could carry a late night without asking to go home. She signed her initials smaller than usual. She remembered that, the smallness, as if a smaller signature would own less of what it cleared.

It owned all of it. The fund emptied fourteen months later. Two hundred people she never met lost their pensions, one man among them the thing he had built across forty years, and her small initials sat at the bottom of the page that let it go. No one ever came for her. She had the names of the people it emptied in a document she kept and did not open. Her own bank statements lived in the same folder, also unopened.

The hand that drew Sallowmere’s tier-three floor was the same hand she had seen, from the same firm, in the year she did not think about.

The coffee had gone cold, and she drank it standing at the counter, then rinsed the mug and went back to the table.

The sensible read was coincidence. A firm forms a lot of shells. An associate has a style. Two clients years apart can share a drafter the way two houses can share a plumber. Naomi said it to herself in the partner’s reassuring voice and did not believe a word of it. Sallowmere’s third tier was not a company hiding money from a spouse or a partner or the ordinary tax. It was built to move. Money went in one door dressed as one thing and came out another door dressed as another, and the dressing was expensive. Nobody paid for dressing like that unless the people looking for the money were the kind who hurt you when they found it.

She found one of the movements before she meant to look for it. A payment left a tier-two account in March, nine thousand one hundred and change, dressed as a consulting retainer, and landed four days later in the tier-three holding as a repayment on a loan the holding company had no record of ever taking. The memo line on the way out said advisory. The memo line on the way in said principal. Same money, two names, four days and one border of the chart between them, and nobody advising anyone about anything. She set the two statements side by side and ticked the dates in pencil, out on the twelfth, in on the sixteenth. In between, the money had rested somewhere the production did not cover. She wrote the gap up as an open item.

Then she went looking for April and found it: nine thousand four hundred and six out on the ninth, in on the thirteenth, advisory going out and principal coming home. May was the same with a different number. June had two. By the time she had six months ticked in pencil down the margin, the shape did not need her to argue for it. She stopped at six because the coffee was gone and her hand had started making the ticks too neat.

What sat wrong under her ribs was the patience of it. The amounts stayed just below the lines that make a bank pick up a phone, month after month, steady as rent. Greedy money made mistakes. In her experience, money this patient had somewhere it needed to be and somebody making sure it got there.

It was one in the morning. The fee that would clear her mother’s mortgage was attached to the exact thing she had left her whole career to get away from. The mortgage and the mistake, on the same piece of paper. She almost laughed. Six years she had audited everyone’s books but her own, and the first client to bring her real money had brought her past with him, gift-wrapped, care of D. Osei.

She looked up Osei because she looked up everyone. There was not much. No firm website, only a bar listing and an office address downtown. A bar number, current. A defense practice that had barely started before it went quiet, no courtroom appearances since, which for a criminal lawyer meant a different kind of work. One photograph, from a charity thing: a man at the edge of the frame in a suit that fit him the way suits fit men who have decided the suit is part of the job. He was not looking at the camera. He was looking at the exits, the way her father did in restaurants, except her father did it from old habit, and she could not tell from one photograph what this man did it from. He was a big man holding himself small, wide through the shoulders and careful with it. Dark brown skin. A jaw shaved that morning and already deciding otherwise, hair cut close, gray coming in at the temples where he had not bothered to fight it. One hand hung loose. The other held a glass, the knuckles healed over more than once, an old story the suit was there to keep quiet. He had good hands, which was not on her list. His face was built for stillness. Practiced, she thought, and expensive, like a set of books that reconciled too cleanly.

She filed him under unverified, saved the photo to the same folder as the chart scans, and went back to the box.

There was a note in the box that did not belong with an accountant’s paper. One page, cream stock, no letterhead, three lines in an upright hand.

Ms. Reyes. Everything you asked for is enclosed, and everything you did not ask for is available on request. I am the person you call when a document is missing. D.O.

She read it four times. Real ink off a real nib, the pressure heaviest on the word missing. Tilted against the lamp, the strokes showed through the back of the page. It was courteous to the point of a threat, or a threat to the point of courtesy, and she could not make it settle into one or the other. She suspected that was the design. She set the note on the tier-three corner of the chart. Then she moved it to the far end of the table, face down.

She started the request list: bank statements to each cutoff, wire logs, board minutes, engagement letters with outside counsel. On the fourth line she wrote Pellam and Croft, all formation files, tier three, and looked at it, and crossed it out so hard the pen went through. A request that plain told the other side what you suspected before you had proved it. She would ask for the engagement letters with outside counsel and let the firm’s name come out of the pile on its own. She wrote that down too, small, in the margin, where only she would read it.

The phone buzzed. Bel, who should have been asleep, sent a picture of a cat that was not hers with the caption this is you at finals. Naomi typed back go to bed, and then, because Bel would hear the flatness in three words, added a line about the ceramics class, a small true thing to prove she was fine. Bel sent back a heart and nothing else. Naomi looked at the heart for a while before she put the phone away.

Her mother did not know about the mortgage inside the fee, and would not be told. Neither would her father, Sang, who left the bills to her mother and the engines to himself. Her father had already lost one retirement to a firm that signed off on books it should have flagged. Nobody in the family said that out loud either. If Naomi paid off the second mortgage, her mother would ask where the money came from, and Naomi would say work, and her mother would look at her the way she looked at a pot on too high a flame, and ask nothing else, and that would be the end of it. She would take the fee and keep the client’s name out of her mother’s kitchen.

At two she wrote to Osei. Production received. Index to follow. Requests to follow. She read it back, took out thank you, and sent it. Then she made the decision she had already made: she would take the job and do it exactly, to the floor, and she would not tell the client that the drafter of his third tier had her initials on his work in another life. She planned the work from the bank in. Statements first, three years, every account in the production, then every account the production implied and did not include. Then the transfers, mapped and dated, both memo lines for every movement. Sampling was for auditors with partners to answer to. She would read everything. If the money went somewhere that made her report say a name, it would say the name. She would deal with her own initials when the paper made her, and not one day before.

She capped the pen and turned off the lamp. In the dark the bowl was an outline on the sill and the chart was only paper.

She did not sleep. She lay in the clean dark and did the sums again, the mortgage balance, the fee, the retainer, what would be left for her mother and what would be left for rent. Somewhere around four it came to her that she had agreed to spend three months inside a machine built by dangerous people, alone, with her name the only one on the door.

She got up before the alarm, initialed the first page of the formation documents, dated it, and began.
